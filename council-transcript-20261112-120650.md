# LLM Council Transcript — AZ-104 Study Strategy
**Date:** 2026-11-12  
**Session:** Should I abandon "save Shireen" and start Shireen Khan tests now?

---

## Original Question
"Should I stop saving Shireen Khan's AZ-104 practice exams and just start doing them now?"

**User clarification:** The strategy was not "save for the final weeks" — it was "exhaust all other resources first (AI simulator + Alan Rodrigues remaining tests), THEN do Shireen last."

---

## Framed Question
Candidate is preparing for AZ-104 (Microsoft Azure Administrator) exam, sitting around December 5–8, 2026 — approximately 24 days away. They had been strategically "saving" Shireen Khan's Udemy practice tests, planning to exhaust all other resources first (AI simulator + Alan Rodrigues remaining tests), THEN do Shireen last. They believed the course had ~3–4 short tests, but just discovered it has 6 full tests × ~90 questions = ~530 total high-difficulty questions. They've completed: Alan Rodrigues Practice Test 01 (73.8%) and PT02 (78.4%), and have a custom AI-built 195-question simulator (3 exams × 65 questions) available. The question: Should they abandon the "exhaust everything else first, then Shireen" strategy and start doing Shireen Khan tests immediately as their primary practice resource?

---

## Advisor Responses

### The Contrarian
The "save Shireen" strategy was never strategic — it was procrastination dressed up as planning. But here's the flaw nobody's naming: abandoning it now doesn't automatically fix the problem.

530 questions across 24 days sounds manageable until you do the math honestly. That's ~22 questions/day if you do *nothing else* — no review, no gap-filling, no re-reads. Meaningful practice requires reviewing every wrong answer, understanding why, and reinforcing weak domains. Realistically, one full 90-question Shireen test plus proper review takes 3–4 hours minimum. You can't sprint through 6 of those plus Alan Rodrigues leftovers plus the AI simulator without the later tests becoming mechanical checkbox exercises that teach you nothing.

The deeper flaw: more questions ≠ better retention. If you're burning through Shireen tests at a pace that doesn't allow consolidation, you're just burning your best resource for the dopamine hit of "completing" something. You'll arrive at exam day having seen the questions but not learned from them.

The real question nobody's asking: what were your wrong answers on Alan PT01 and PT02, and have you actually fixed those gaps? A 73–78% score leaves a 22–26% failure zone unaddressed. Piling on 530 more questions before closing those gaps compounds the problem.

**What you should actually do:** Stop debating sequencing and spend two days auditing your PT01/PT02 mistakes by domain. Then decide which resource targets your weakest domains hardest. That determines your sequence — not arbitrary "save the best for last" logic.

### The First Principles Thinker
Strip away the meta-strategy entirely. What are you actually trying to accomplish? **Pass AZ-104 on December 5–8.** That's it. Everything else is scaffolding.

Now ask: what does "passing" require? A score above Microsoft's cut threshold (~700/1000), achieved by correctly answering questions on exam day — not by having preserved a pristine set of untouched practice tests.

Here's the flawed assumption baked into the entire "save Shireen" strategy: that unseen questions are intrinsically more valuable than reviewed ones. **They are not.** What matters is whether you've internalized the knowledge domain, not whether the specific question wording is novel. Practice tests are diagnostic instruments, not finite ammunition. Saving them "for the end" only makes sense if your goal is to simulate exam conditions once — but you have 24 days and multiple resources. You can run a cold simulation with Shireen now, learn from every wrong answer, review the domains, then run another test later.

The real question isn't "Shireen now or Shireen later?" It's: "Which resource closes my weakest knowledge gaps fastest?"

If Shireen's tests are genuinely harder and more comprehensive, they are your best diagnostic tool. Using your weakest mirror last — when there's no time to fix what it reveals — is backwards. Diagnostic value is highest when you still have time to course-correct.

**Start Shireen immediately.** Use her tests as the primary knowledge-gap detector. Use Alan Rodrigues and your AI simulator for reinforcement on weak domains. Save one or two Shireen tests for final cold-run simulation within 3 days of the exam.

The "save it for last" instinct is about psychological comfort, not optimal learning. Discard it.

### The Expansionist
The "save Shireen" strategy was built on scarcity that doesn't exist. Now that you know you have 530 questions, the whole framing flips — this isn't a resource to protect, it's a *massive* volume advantage most AZ-104 candidates don't have. Stop treating it like a precious last resort and start treating it like a training ground.

Here's what everyone else is missing: 530 high-difficulty questions across 24 days is roughly 22 questions per day. That's not an emergency — that's a system. You could run Shireen questions daily as your primary reps, use Alan Rodrigues as a periodic benchmark, and weave in your AI simulator for gap-targeted drilling. That's three complementary feedback loops running simultaneously, not sequentially.

Sequential strategy made sense when Shireen was 3–4 short tests. At full scale, sequential means you spend the first two weeks on lower-density material and only hit the richest resource when you have the least time to course-correct. That's backwards.

The bigger upside: Shireen's high difficulty isn't a reason to delay — it's a reason to frontload. Hard questions expose unknown unknowns earlier. Every wrong answer in week one is a week of learning. Every wrong answer in week three is just anxiety.

Blow up the sequencing plan entirely. Start Shireen now, run it as your backbone, and let the other resources fill in around it. The candidate who finishes all 530 Shireen questions with a week to spare is in a completely different position than the one who cracks them open on day 20.

### The Outsider
The "save Shireen" strategy sounds like it made sense at the time, but it was built on a factual error — you thought you were preserving a small resource, but you were actually sitting on your largest one. The original logic is void. You can't optimize around a misunderstanding.

Here's what I see from the outside: you have 24 days and roughly 800+ practice questions available across all resources. The only question that actually matters is *how many quality reps can you get before exam day?* Sequencing is a secondary concern. Volume and review quality are primary.

One thing that jumps out to me as an outsider: you're treating practice tests like a finite fuel tank to be rationed. But if the goal is exam readiness, you want *maximum exposure to hard questions as early as possible*, so you have time to actually learn from wrong answers. Saving hard questions for the end means you identify gaps too late to fix them.

Start Shireen now. Here's why the "save it for last" framing is backwards: you don't want your first encounter with a hard question to be under exam conditions. You want it to be three weeks ago, when you still had time to revisit the concept.

The AI simulator is probably the lowest-stakes resource — use it for warm-up or gap-filling, not as a primary sequence anchor.

Simple reframe: treat every day without hard practice questions as a wasted diagnostic opportunity. You've been holding the diagnostic tool in your hands the whole time.

### The Executor
Drop the "save Shireen" strategy. Right now. Here's the actual plan:

**Monday morning action**: Open Shireen Khan Test 1. Take it. That's your new primary resource.

Here's why this is the only executable path: You have 24 days and 530 high-difficulty questions sitting untouched. The original sequencing logic assumed Shireen was 3–4 short tests — a finishing sprint. At 6 × 90 questions, it's a full training regime on its own. You cannot sprint through 530 questions in the final week. The math doesn't work.

**What you actually do over 24 days:**
- Days 1–18: Rotate through Shireen's 6 tests (one every ~3 days). After each test, spend 1 day reviewing every wrong answer — not skimming, actually looking up the topic.
- Days 19–22: Hit your AI simulator (195 questions over 3 sessions). It's lower difficulty, so it now works as a confidence calibration tool, not primary prep.
- Days 23–24: Alan Rodrigues PT01/PT02 for final weak-spot identification. You've already done them, so a quick rescan of your wrong answers takes 2–3 hours, not full retakes.

Alan Rodrigues' tests are done — stop treating them as a future resource.

The only question you need to answer today: What time are you sitting down to take Shireen Test 1? Not "this week." Today or tomorrow. Everything else is planning theater.

530 questions at your current pace requires starting immediately. There is no sequencing strategy that makes waiting correct.

## Chairman's Verdict

### Where the Council Agrees
**The "save Shireen" strategy is void — abandon it immediately.** Every advisor reached this independently and from different angles. The original logic was built on a factual error (3–4 short tests vs. 6 × 90 questions). You cannot optimize around a premise that was false. There is no version of the sequencing debate where waiting to start Shireen is correct.

**Hard questions belong at the front, not the back.** This was unanimous and well-reasoned. A wrong answer in week one gives you three weeks to close the gap. A wrong answer in week three gives you anxiety. Diagnostic value is highest when time to course-correct is longest. Saving your most rigorous resource for last is psychologically comfortable and strategically backwards.

**Unseen questions are not intrinsically more valuable than reviewed ones.** The "preserve novelty" instinct feels rational but isn't. What matters on exam day is whether you've internalized the knowledge domain — not whether the question wording is fresh. Practice tests are diagnostic instruments, not finite ammunition.

### Where the Council Clashes
**How much to sweat the wrong answers from PT01/PT02 before moving forward.**

The Contrarian argues you should stop everything and spend two days auditing your PT01/PT02 mistake domains before touching Shireen. The Executor says PT01/PT02 are done — open Shireen Test 1 today.

Both are right about something. The Contrarian is correct that a 73–78% score means a 22–26% failure zone that is currently unaddressed. The Executor is correct that endless gap analysis before starting your best resource is its own form of procrastination. The resolution: spend 30–60 minutes tonight reviewing wrong-answer domains by category, flag the two or three weakest, then start Shireen Test 1 tomorrow. Don't let gap analysis become the new delay tactic.

**How manageable "22 questions/day" actually is.**

The Expansionist called it "not an emergency — that's a system." The Contrarian, and all five peer reviewers, flagged this as dangerously optimistic. One 90-question Shireen test plus proper wrong-answer review is 3–4 hours minimum. Take the pessimists' side here: if you don't have 3–4 hours per day available, volume prioritization beats volume completion. Doing fewer tests with thorough review beats doing all six at a sprint.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught
**Is the exam actually booked?** Three of five peer reviewers flagged this and every advisor missed it. "December 5–8" is a four-day window, not a confirmed date. If the slot isn't locked, the urgency calculus is undefined — and booking it is the highest-leverage action available.

**How many hours per day can you actually study?** Not one advisor asked. 530 questions over 24 days is a manageable system if you have 3–4 hours/day. It's an impossible ask if you have 1–2. If you're constrained, prioritization (which Shireen tests to take) beats volume (attempting all six).

**AZ-104 content updates.** One reviewer flagged that AZ-104 had a significant content refresh in 2023. If Shireen Khan's question bank or Alan Rodrigues' tests predate those updates, high-volume grinding through outdated material can produce false confidence. Verify that your resources are current before treating any score as a reliable signal.

**Burnout risk.** A 24-day intensive sprint with 3–4 hours/day study on top of normal life obligations has a real cognitive load ceiling. No advisor modeled fatigue. Build in at least one light day per week intentionally, or you'll get one accidentally — at a worse time.

### The Recommendation
**Start Shireen Khan Test 1 tomorrow. Make it your primary resource. Reserve Tests 5 and 6 for cold-run simulations in the final three days before the exam.**

Here is the actual plan:
- **Tonight (30–60 min):** Review your PT01/PT02 wrong answers by domain. Identify your two or three weakest areas. Write them down. This is not a two-day audit — it is one focused pass to prime your attention for Shireen.
- **Days 1–18:** Work through Shireen Tests 1–4, one test every three to four days. After each test: one full review session on wrong answers, with particular attention to your pre-identified weak domains. Do not skip review to move faster.
- **Days 19–21:** Use your AI simulator (195 questions over three sessions) as targeted drilling on whatever domains Shireen has revealed as persistent weaknesses. It's lower difficulty, so it now functions as confidence calibration, not primary prep.
- **Days 22–24:** Shireen Tests 5 and 6, taken cold, back to back. These are your exam simulations. Score them honestly and do light review only — no cramming new material.
- **Alan Rodrigues remaining tests:** Only if you have surplus time. They are not the priority.

If your daily study window is under two hours, cut the AI simulator entirely and focus exclusively on Shireen tests with full review. Depth over volume.

### The One Thing to Do First
**Book your exam slot for a specific date — right now, before you open any practice test.**

A fixed date transforms this from an abstract planning problem into a real countdown. Every other decision (which test first, how much review time, when to simulate) becomes easier once the endpoint is locked. If the slot is already booked, skip this — and your one thing is: open Shireen Test 1 tomorrow morning and do not rationalize any reason to delay it further.
